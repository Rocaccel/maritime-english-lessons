# Main Parts of a Vessel. Ship's Hull

The main parts of a vessel are: the hull, the engines, the propellers, the rudder.

The main body of a ship is called the hull. The hull is the watertight shell of a ship. It is divided into a number of horizontal surfaces called decks.

Hulls can be of 3 shapes: (A) S-bottom hull, (B) hard and (C) soft chine hull. Bulkheads are walls built between the decks, forming compartments. Each compartment has special doors that, when closed, make it watertight. Those dividing up compartments are known as tween decks on which the cargo rests. If water floods one compartment because of an accident, closing the doors will trap the water there and prevent it from flooding other compartments. Watertight compartments enable a ship to float even with a hole in her hull.

Hulls have a pointed bow to knife swiftly through the water. Most hulls also have a rounded stern, which helps the water close smoothly behind as the ship cuts through the water. The hull is divided into three parts: fore end, midships and after end.

The hull contains: the engine-room, cargo space and a number of tanks.

In dry cargo ships the cargo space is divided into holds, in liquid cargo ships it is divided into tanks. At the fore end of the hull are the fore peak tanks and at the after end are the after peak tanks. They are used for fresh water and water ballast. The space between the holds and the bottom of the hull contains double bottom tanks. These are used for ballast water and fuel.

The deck at the top of the hull is called the main deck. All the structures above the main deck make up the superstructure. The forecastle is at the fore end. The bridge superstructure and the poop are combined at the after end.

The engine is fitted near the bottom of the ship in the engine-room. The engine drives a propeller at the stern of a ship. The engines of most ships can be steam turbines, gas turbines or diesel engines.

The propellers, also called screws, move a ship through the water. The propeller is bolted to the end of the shaft. Most propellers have four blades. As a propeller turns, it screws itself through the water and so pushes the ship forward. Most small ships have one propeller. Many larger vessels have two propellers and very big ships have four. Some ships have an extra propeller called a bow thruster. A bow thruster, which is located in the bow, turns the ship more rapidly than stern propellers alone.

The rudder is a large flat piece of metal that steers a ship. It is hinged to the stern and so can be swung like a door. The rudder is connected to the helm (steering wheel) on the ship's bridge.

Funnels are situated near the bridge to discharge smoke and exhaust fumes. Anchors are on the left and right sides of the bow.

Power-driven winches operate like cranes for loading and discharging the cargo. They are also used to raise or lower the anchors and to bring in or let out the mooring lines used to tie vessels at a pier.

Derricks, fitted to the masts, are necessary for cargo operations. Derricks are operated by winches. The cargo is loaded and discharged to the holds through the cargo hatches.

Modern ships also have high-speed pumps to pump out ballast water or pump up seawater in case of fire. Radiotelegraph equipment keeps ships in constant touch with the rest of the world.
